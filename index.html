What Is a SWOT Analysis?

A SWOT analysis is a structured planning framework that evaluates four dimensions of any venture, project, team or career decision: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats. Strengths and weaknesses are internal factors — things you control, such as your skills, resources, brand reputation or cost structure. Opportunities and threats are external factors — market trends, competitor moves, regulation and economic shifts that you must respond to but cannot directly control. The framework dates back to management research at Stanford in the 1960s and 1970s, and it has survived for a simple reason: it forces honest, categorized thinking before decisions get made.

A good SWOT analysis template does more than list bullet points. It separates what is true today (strengths and weaknesses) from what could be true tomorrow (opportunities and threats), and it keeps each quadrant specific enough to act on. "Good team" is not a useful strength; "a support team that resolves 94% of tickets within four hours" is. The more concrete each sticky note on your board, the more valuable the strategies that come out of it later.

The Four Quadrants, Done Right

Strengths: your unfair advantages

Strengths are internal, positive and present-tense. Ask: what do customers praise us for? What do we do cheaper, faster or better than alternatives? What assets — data, relationships, patents, cash, talent — would be hard for a rival to copy? Write each strength as one observable fact per note. If a strength cannot be observed or measured by someone outside your team, rewrite it until it can.

Weaknesses: the honest inventory

Weaknesses are internal, negative and present-tense. This quadrant is where most SWOT exercises fail, because teams soften the truth. Ask the uncomfortable questions: where do we lose deals, what breaks under load, which skills are missing, what do one-star reviews keep repeating? A weakness phrased as "we could improve our marketing" is useless; "we have zero inbound pipeline and rely on two referral partners" is a weakness you can actually fix.

Opportunities: where the wind is blowing

Opportunities are external and positive. Look at market growth, underserved segments, regulatory changes, new distribution channels, technology shifts and competitor stumbles. The test of a real opportunity is that it exists whether or not you act on it — a rising tide you can choose to surf. Rank opportunities by size and by fit: a giant market you cannot serve is a distraction, not an opportunity.

Threats: what could sink the plan

Threats are external and negative: new entrants, price wars, platform dependency, regulation, supply-chain shocks, changing customer habits. Name each threat specifically and, where possible, note its likelihood and timing. "AI disruption" is vague; "two competitors launched AI-assisted onboarding that cuts setup time from days to minutes" is a threat you can strategize against.

The Problem with Most SWOTs: Lists That Go Nowhere

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the classic SWOT analysis template: most teams fill the four boxes, admire the result for ten minutes, and then go back to doing whatever they were already doing. A SWOT on its own is descriptive, not prescriptive. It tells you where you stand, but not where to step. Consultants have a name for this failure mode — the "laundry list" SWOT — and it is the reason many founders quietly consider SWOT a waste of time.

The fix is a lesser-known companion framework called the TOWS matrix, developed by Heinz Weihrich in 1982. TOWS takes the exact same four quadrants and flips the question: instead of "what are our factors?", it asks "what do our factors tell us to do?" Every cell of the TOWS matrix pairs an internal factor with an external one and produces a strategy. That pairing step is the entire difference between a SWOT that decorates a slide deck and a SWOT that drives a roadmap.

The TOWS Matrix: Turning Factors into Strategy

The TOWS matrix template crosses the internal quadrants (Strengths, Weaknesses) with the external quadrants (Opportunities, Threats) to produce four strategy types. Each strategy answers a specific question, and together they cover offense, improvement, defense and survival.

SO strategies (Maxi-Maxi): play offense

SO strategies ask: which strength helps us capture which opportunity? These are your highest-confidence moves — you are deploying proven advantages against favorable conditions. Example: a bakery with a loyal neighborhood following (strength) notices offices reopening nearby (opportunity); the SO strategy is a corporate catering subscription pitched first to regulars who work in those offices. When Quadra's guided worksheet asks "which strength will help you capture this opportunity?", it is building your SO list one pairing at a time.

WO strategies (Mini-Maxi): fix to grow

WO strategies ask: which weakness must we fix to seize which opportunity? These are improvement bets — you invest in removing an internal limitation because an external prize justifies it. Example: a freelancer with no online presence (weakness) sees startups hiring fractional designers (opportunity); the WO strategy is to ship a portfolio site and two case studies within 30 days, then pitch ten startups a week. WO strategies are often the highest-leverage items on the board, because the weakness was already costing you and the opportunity gives the fix a deadline.

ST strategies (Maxi-Mini): build the moat

ST strategies ask: which strength shields us against which threat? These are defensive moves that turn advantages into protection. Example: a SaaS company with best-in-class onboarding (strength) faces a well-funded competitor entering the market (threat); the ST strategy is to double down on migration concierge and annual contracts that raise switching costs before the competitor gains traction. Good ST strategies convert "we hope they don't attack" into "let them try".

WT strategies (Mini-Mini): survive and stabilize

WT strategies ask: which weakness makes which threat most dangerous, and how do we reduce that exposure? These are damage-control moves: retrenchment, partnerships, hedging, or orderly exits from losing positions. Example: a retailer dependent on one supplier (weakness) faces rising import tariffs (threat); the WT strategy is to qualify a domestic backup supplier within a quarter. Nobody enjoys WT planning, but teams that skip it are the ones blindsided later.

From Strategies to Action: Impact × Effort Prioritization

A finished TOWS matrix can easily hold a dozen strategies, and you cannot execute twelve things at once. The classic prioritization tool is the impact–effort matrix: score each strategy on expected impact (1–5) and required effort (1–5), then sort by the ratio of impact to effort. Quadra computes this automatically and drops every strategy into one of four buckets:

  • Quick Wins (high impact, low effort) — do these first. They build momentum and fund harder work.
  • Major Projects (high impact, high effort) — plan carefully, assign owners, break into milestones.
  • Fill-Ins (low impact, low effort) — tackle in spare cycles; nice polish, not strategy.
  • Time Sinks (low impact, high effort) — avoid or defer. Saying no here is the strategy.

Two practical tips make the voting honest. First, score impact on outcomes that matter to the business — revenue, retention, cost — not on how exciting the idea feels. Second, score effort in real units: person-weeks, cash outlay, calendar time. When a "quick win" turns out to need three teams and a quarter, it was never a quick win; the matrix just exposed it early, which is exactly its job.

Common SWOT Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

  • Vague entries. "Good brand" helps nobody. Rewrite until each note names something observable.
  • Mixing internal and external. A competitor's pricing is not your weakness — it is a threat. Your slow release cycle is the weakness. Keep the axes clean or the pairings break.
  • Too many notes. Ten crisp notes per quadrant beat thirty fuzzy ones. Merge duplicates ruthlessly.
  • Skipping the TOWS step. A SWOT without pairings is a poster. Force every opportunity and every threat through at least one strategy question.
  • Treating it as one-and-done. Revisit the board quarterly. Strengths decay, threats evolve, and last quarter's WT survival move may be this quarter's SO offensive.
  • Ranking by gut feel. Use the impact × effort vote. It takes five minutes and kills endless debate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a SWOT analysis and a TOWS matrix?

A SWOT analysis lists your internal strengths and weaknesses alongside external opportunities and threats. A TOWS matrix goes one step further: it pairs those factors to generate strategies — SO (use strengths to capture opportunities), WO (fix weaknesses to seize opportunities), ST (use strengths to defend against threats) and WT (minimize weaknesses and avoid threats). Think of SWOT as the diagnosis and TOWS as the prescription.

How many items should I put in each SWOT quadrant?

Aim for 3–8 specific notes per quadrant. Fewer than three and you are probably missing something important; more than ten and the matrix becomes noisy. One concrete idea per sticky note works best — merge duplicates and rewrite vague entries until they name something observable.

Can you give an example of an SO strategy from a TOWS matrix?

Sure. If a bakery's strength is "loyal regulars who visit daily" and the opportunity is "offices reopening nearby", the SO strategy is: use the loyal regulars to capture the office reopening — for example, launching a corporate catering subscription pitched first to regulars who work in those offices.
